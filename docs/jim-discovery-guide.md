# Jim Discovery: Working Session Guide

Date: 2026-08-24
Purpose: the script for the discovery sessions with Jim. Roadmap step 7 swaps Meridian's stand-in for his real system, and the seam contract is derived from his real workflow, never invented on our side. This guide turns the seam document's section 4 into a session plan. The seam document stays the authority; if an answer here conflicts with it, the finding is recorded before anything is designed around it.

How to use it: work backward from finished projects. Five to ten completed artboards across different artists, stages, and technical conditions. For each project, walk the eight areas below in order. Write down what he says in his words. Do not propose fields, formats, or process changes in the session; the contract is written afterward from the notes.

One rule for the notetaker: every answer is evidence. "It depends" is an answer; write down what it depends on. A shrug is an answer; it means the thing lives in his head and the contract has to leave it there.

## Per project, eight areas

**1. What arrived.** Everything he received to start this job, in whatever form: call, text, email, deck, diagram, verbal note at a bar. Which parts were creative, which technical, which artist context. What he wishes had arrived and did not.

**2. What he already knew.** What he inferred or carried in from knowing the artist, the venue class, or live production generally. Which decisions were made before his system was touched. This is the part Meridian's brief has to carry for a new artist, so push for specifics: name the decision, ask what fact drove it.

**3. What his system consumed.** What actually enters the tools: one brief, staged inputs, files, prompts. What structured data exists anywhere (spreadsheets, templates, naming conventions count). Which intermediate artifacts get made, which are reused across jobs, which are thrown away.

**4. How the artboard is made.** Composite or single output. What software and code. Which parts are deterministic, which generative, which hand-adjusted. Which layout rules never change and which vary per job. How PIP and stage geometry are represented.

**5. Where the technical truth lives.** Hardware, software, device, stage, and screen profiles: where are they written down, if anywhere. How resolutions and codecs are represented. Which checks are rules he could state and which are judgment he applies by eye. Which of it is reusable across clients. Flag anything he considers his own IP; Meridian holds a reference, not a copy.

**6. What comes out.** The artboard format, exactly. Whether his system can produce, today or cheaply: a machine-readable sidecar, a concept summary, a job identifier, a version number, a manifest. How the file gets to the client today.

**7. How revisions arrive and are applied.** The form feedback takes today. Whether prior state is preserved when he revises. Whether a revision can be tied to a specific earlier version. What happens when feedback is about one part of the picture: how does he know which part, and would a named region on the instruction help or annoy. This is the region-anchor question from the seam document; ask it against a real revision from the project on the table.

**8. From approved artboard to finished media.** What carries forward automatically, what gets re-entered by hand, where drift between approved and delivered has actually happened. Which production steps could reference an immutable approved version if one existed. What he checks before delivery.

## Once, not per project

- **Receipts.** When something arrives from a client today, does anything acknowledge it? Would his side send back "received, job X, brief version N" if receiving were structured? This is the seam document's receipt question; Meridian's client review shows the receipt, so the answer shapes a screen.
- **Identity and versions.** Does any identifier survive from request to delivery today? What would he naturally call a job?
- **Retry and failure.** When something goes wrong mid-job today, how does he find out and what does he do? What should Meridian do if it sends the same brief twice?
- **The brief itself.** Show him a real frozen Meridian brief from the pilot Scene, markdown form. Where would he stop reading? What is missing, what is noise, what order would he want? The working assumption in the seam document, concrete facts early and latitude after, gets tested here against his eyes, not asserted.
- **What he never wants.** The seam document promises Meridian will not ask his system to expose prompting, hold client review, or reshape around our assumptions. Say that out loud and ask what else belongs on the list.

## What the notes become

Three documents, written after the sessions, not during: corrections to the seam document's payload shapes, marked against his words; a list of what needs hardening on his side, drawn from section 5 of the seam document with items struck or added by evidence; and the adapter specification, written only from manual translations logged during the pilot. Nothing is automated until repeated work shows a stable contract.

## What not to do in the room

Do not design. Do not promise fields. Do not show him Meridian screens before area 8 is done for at least three projects, or every answer afterward describes our product instead of his workflow. Do not treat the stand-in's shape as a suggestion; it is scaffolding and it dies at step 7.
